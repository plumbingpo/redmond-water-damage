Even though the heater itself contains 40 to 80 gallons, the leak is not capped at that amount. Because the supply line feeds the tank, a rusted out bottom drains and refills at the same pace. Nothing stops the flow except shutting the cold water valve. Where the unit sits decides the damage: a garage floor, a hallway closet or, worst of all, an attic above finished rooms.

Do This First

Before We Arrive

  1. Turn Off the Heat SourceElectric units get shut down at the breaker. With a gas heater, the knob on the control goes to the off position. A burner or heating element must not stay on once the tank is empty.
  2. Close the Cold SupplyFind the cold water shutoff above the heater and turn it clockwise. If it is stuck or leaking, shut off the main water valve for the house.
  3. Watch for Hot WaterTank water is hot enough to scald. Pets and children should stay clear, and nobody should walk into pooled water before it cools down.
  4. Empty the Tank OutsideWhen the drain valve is within reach, connect a garden hose and lead it to a floor drain or outdoors, which keeps the rest of the water off the floor.
  5. Move Stored ItemsLift boxes, rugs and anything absorbent off the wet floor. Fabric and cardboard absorb water fast, then keep it pressed to the concrete and walls.
Coverage

Insurance and Water Heater Leak Cleanup

A sudden tank failure is often treated as accidental and sudden, so the resulting damage to belongings, floors and walls is often covered. Replacing the heater is usually not included, because a tank wearing out from age counts as maintenance. Rust trails, old staining or other evidence that the leak was long-term can lead an adjuster to reduce or deny the claim, since gradual leaks usually fall outside coverage. Check your policy, photograph the heater and the damage, and keep the old unit until the claim is settled.

Why It Happens

Why This Happens

  • Drain pans beneath closet or attic units that rust through or clog and then spill over.
  • Years of sediment buildup and corrosion wearing a hole in the base of an old steel tank.
  • Loose plumbing connections left after a recent installation or repair.
  • Flex connectors or inlet and outlet fittings at the top of the heater that have corroded.
  • The relief valve for temperature and pressure opening, or failing, and letting water out.
What Is at Risk

Where the Damage Spreads

Platform and Subfloor

The wooden stand or subfloor under the tank takes the most water. Rot can weaken it, a real concern once a full new tank sits on top.

Adjacent Room Flooring

From a heater closet, the leak slips beneath the door and reaches hallway hardwood, laminate or carpet. Hot water speeds up swelling in wood flooring and loosens carpet backing.

Closet Walls

Drywall surrounding a heater closet inside the house wicks water up and lets it through to the neighboring rooms. The lower wall remains wet on the inside, and the baseboards swell.

Ceilings Under an Attic Heater

A heater in the attic sends water through insulation onto the ceiling below. Soaked insulation loses its insulating value and gets heavy enough to collapse the drywall ceiling.
